dating: |
  Arrian dates the fall of Tyre to the Athenian month Hecatombaion in the archonship of Anicetus [[cite:tyre-falls]], which is July or August of 332 BC, and the battle of Issus to the month Maimacterion of the archon-year before, about November 333 BC [[cite:issus-date]]. For the conversion of these two dates see A. B. Bosworth, *A Historical Commentary on Arrian's History of Alexander*, vol. 1 (1980), which covers books 1–3. Arrian puts Alexander's death in the 114th Olympiad, in the archonship of Hegesias, which is 323 BC [[cite:death]]. He wrote in the second century AD, but he worked from the histories of Ptolemy and Aristobulus, who served in the campaign [[cite:preface]].

  The visit to Jerusalem is another matter. Josephus tells it about AD 93, more than four hundred years after the event [[cite:jaddua]]. Arrian does not mention the Jews, and he has Alexander march from Gaza straight to Egypt, reaching Pelusium on the seventh day [[cite:egypt]]. The story also has Alexander shown the book of Daniel, which many scholars date to the second century BC (see [[event:daniel-in-babylon]]). Most historians treat the visit as a later legend: see Shaye J. D. Cohen, "Alexander the Great and Jaddus the High Priest According to Josephus", *AJS Review* 7–8 (1982–83), pp. 41–68. That Judea came under Alexander in 332 BC with the rest of the region is not in doubt [[cite:gaza]].

  1 Maccabees, cited here for Alexander's successors, is counted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church, as defined at Trent [[cite:trent]], and by the Orthodox Synod of Jerusalem of 1672 [[cite:dositheus]]. Protestant churches do not count it as canonical Scripture; the Church of England lists it among the books that are read "for example of life and instruction of manners" but not used to establish doctrine [[cite:articles]].

In the autumn of 333 BC [[person:alexander-the-great]], king of Macedon, defeated the army of the Persian king Darius III at Issus in Cilicia. Darius fled, and his mother, wife and children were taken in his camp [[cite:issus]]. Persia had ruled Judah for two centuries, since [[person:cyrus-ii|Cyrus]] took [[place:babylon]] (see [[event:fall-of-babylon]]).

Alexander marched down the Phoenician coast. Byblos and Sidon surrendered [[cite:tyre-refuses]]. [[place:tyre]], a walled city on an island [[cite:mole]], offered to obey him but would not let him enter to sacrifice to its god, whom Arrian calls the Tyrian Heracles [[cite:tyre-refuses]]. Alexander built a mole from the mainland out to the island [[cite:mole]]. The city fell in the summer of 332 BC. Arrian counts about 8,000 Tyrians killed and some 30,000 people sold into slavery [[cite:tyre-falls]]. [[place:gaza]] also resisted and was stormed; its men died fighting and its women and children were sold [[cite:gaza]]. [[person:josephus]] gives the length of the two sieges as seven months and two [[cite:sieges]].

The Persian governor of Egypt gave up the country without a battle. Alexander sacrificed at Memphis, sailed down the Nile, and on the coast beside Lake Mareotis marked out the walls, market and temples of a new city, [[place:alexandria]] [[cite:egypt]].

Of Palestine Arrian says at this point that all of it except Gaza had already submitted [[cite:gaza]]. Josephus, writing more than four centuries later, tells a story that Arrian does not have. During the siege of Tyre, he says, the high priest refused Alexander's demand for troops and supplies, because he had given Darius his oath not to bear arms against him [[cite:jaddua-refuses]]. After Gaza, the story goes on, the king marched on [[place:jerusalem]]. The high priest, whom Josephus names Jaddua, was instructed in a dream and went out to meet him with the priests in their vestments and the people in white. Alexander bowed before the divine name on the high priest's headdress and said that he had seen such a figure in a dream in Macedonia. He sacrificed in the temple, was shown the book of Daniel, and granted the Jews the right to live by their ancestral laws and to pay no tribute in the seventh year [[cite:jaddua]].

In 331 BC Alexander defeated Darius for the last time near Arbela [[cite:arbela]], and in 323 BC he died at Babylon [[cite:babylon]] [[cite:death]]. 1 Maccabees opens with his conquests and says that his officers took crowns after him [[bible:1 Macc 1:1-9]], and Daniel pictures a great horn broken and replaced by four [[bible:Dan 8:5-8, 20-22]]. Judea fell first to the Ptolemies of Egypt [[cite:ptolemy]] and later to the Seleucid kings of Syria [[cite:seleucids]]. Greek kings ruled it for the next 190 years, until the Hasmoneans won its independence (see [[event:hasmonean-independence]]). Under them the law of Moses was translated into Greek in Egypt (see [[event:septuagint]]), and the adoption of Greek ways in Jerusalem itself opened the crisis under [[person:antiochus-iv]] [[bible:1 Macc 1:10-15]].
