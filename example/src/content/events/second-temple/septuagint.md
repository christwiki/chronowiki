dating: |
  No contemporary record of the translation survives. The Letter of Aristeas places it under the son of Ptolemy son of Lagus [[cite:captives]], that is Ptolemy II Philadelphus, who reigned from 285 to 246 BC. The Letter is written as if by a Greek courtier of that king, but scholars agree that its author was an Alexandrian Jew of the second century BC, a century or more after the reign he describes: see Benjamin G. Wright III, *The Letter of Aristeas* (2015). Its story is therefore tradition, not a report.

  That the five books of Moses were put into Greek in Egypt in the third century BC is nevertheless the general view. The Greek of the translation matches that of Egyptian papyri of that century: see John A. L. Lee, *A Lexical Study of the Septuagint Version of the Pentateuch* (1983), and Jennifer M. Dines, *The Septuagint* (2004). The date of about 250 BC stands for that judgment.

  Two fixed points come later. The translator of Sirach says that he came to Egypt in the thirty-eighth year of King Euergetes and that the law, the prophets and the other books could by then be compared in Greek and Hebrew [[cite:sirach]]. Only Ptolemy VIII Euergetes reigned so long, and his thirty-eighth year is 132 BC. And fragments of a Greek Deuteronomy copied in Egypt in the second century BC survive [[cite:rylands]]. The remaining books were translated over the following two centuries (see Dines).

After [[person:alexander-the-great]], Egypt was ruled from [[place:alexandria]] by Greek kings, the Ptolemies. The Letter of Aristeas says that the first of them, Ptolemy son of Lagus, brought a hundred thousand Jews into Egypt as captives and soldiers [[cite:captives]].

The same Letter tells how the law of Moses came to be translated. Its author presents himself as an official of [[person:ptolemy-ii]]. Demetrius of Phalerum, in charge of the royal library, tells the king that the laws of the Jews deserve a place in it but must first be translated [[cite:request]]. The king writes to the high priest Eleazar in [[place:jerusalem]], who sends seventy-two elders, six from each tribe, with a copy of the law [[cite:letters]]. The king receives them at Alexandria and bows before the scrolls [[cite:arrival]]. They are taken to a quiet house on the island of Pharos, where they work each day, compare their drafts and agree on a text. The work is finished in seventy-two days [[cite:pharos]]. The translation is read to the Jewish community, which approves it and lays a curse on anyone who alters it [[cite:approval]].

Later writers repeat the story and add to it. [[person:josephus]] retells the Letter at length and speaks of "the seventy elders" [[cite:josephus]]. From that round number the translation takes its name, the Septuagint, "the Seventy". Philo of Alexandria, in the first century AD, says that the translators wrote as if inspired, each using the same words, and that Jews and others still kept a yearly festival on Pharos in memory of the work [[cite:philo]]. About AD 180 the Christian bishop [[person:irenaeus]] tells of seventy elders whom the king kept apart from one another and who all produced the same translation, word for word. He applies the story to all the Scriptures, where the Letter speaks only of the law [[cite:irenaeus]].

The other books followed. The grandson of Ben Sira, who translated his grandfather's book in Egypt late in the second century BC, could already speak of the law, the prophets and the rest of the books as read in Greek, and he remarks that they differ not a little from the original [[cite:sirach]].

The New Testament was written in Greek, and its writers often quote the Old Testament in the wording of the Septuagint. Hebrews cites a psalm in the form "you prepared a body for me" [[bible:Heb 10:5]], which is the reading of the Greek Psalter [[cite:psalm]]; the Hebrew has "You have opened my ears" [[bible:Ps 40:6]]. Irenaeus defends the Septuagint's "virgin" at Isaiah 7:14 against newer Greek versions that read "young woman" [[cite:irenaeus]], the verse Matthew quotes of the birth of [[person:jesus]] [[bible:Matt 1:22-23]].
