## [sources] Every claim is tied to a primary source

A primary source is the text or the object itself: a chronicle, a letter, a law, an inscription, a manuscript, an artifact.

In every narrative the citation follows the sentence it supports and names the passage. One click opens that passage. Citations look like this: [[bible:2 Kings 25:8-10]] for Scripture and [[cite:chronicle]] for other sources. Hovering over the second kind shows what the source is and where it is kept.

Encyclopedias, textbooks and websites that summarise history are not cited. Where a source was written long after the events it describes, the entry says so.

## [confidence] How dates are labelled

Ancient sources rarely state a year. Most dates are worked out from regnal years, synchronisms and later calculation, and some are far more secure than others. Every date therefore carries one of four labels, shown as a mark beside it.

{confidence-list}

Unless a date is firm, the entry has a section called "How this is dated" that names the evidence and says what remains disputed.

## [map] The map

The map is drawn by the site from the public-domain Natural Earth dataset. It shows no modern borders or roads, and its coastlines, lakes and rivers are those of today. Places are named as they were at the time, with the modern name on the place's own page.

A solid pin marks a place whose position is securely known. A hollow, dashed pin marks one that is approximate, fixed only by later tradition, or disputed among scholars; the place's page says which.
